Where they started

Every delivery order was a printed sheet. There was no status until the paper came back. The dispatcher assigned and tracked every driver by hand — by phone and memory — and customers called to ask where their cargo was. Nobody knew for sure.

There was no data at all: no progress tracking, no history, no proof of delivery on file. Growth meant hiring more dispatchers, or saying no to work.

The first step

We didn't pitch a platform. We solved one annoying problem, fast — and every step after that removed work somebody was doing by hand that week.

  • Week 1A script that checks the airport cargo portal, replacing one-by-one manual AWB lookups.
  • Wk 2–4A shared AWB tracker: one web page with every shipment, bulk import, user roles and a daily report.
  • Month 2A driver app with live location and geofencing, so dispatch could see the trucks without calling.

Paper and software ran side by side for most of 2025 until the team trusted it. In January 2026 they switched over completely.

Where AI earns its keep

Only once the data was digital did AI have something to work with. It's applied to specific chores, not bolted on:

  • Reads the inbox. Pre-alert emails and PDFs are parsed into AWB records, so nobody retypes shipment data.
  • Answers questions. Staff ask in plain English; AI writes a read-only, sandboxed query against live data.
  • Talks to customers' AI. A per-customer MCP endpoint lets their own assistants look up their shipments.
Before → after
  • Delivery orders: paper sheets → ~1,000 a month, digital end to end.
  • Driver assignment: dispatcher by phone → live board, rules enforced by the system.
  • Shipment status: customers call and ask → ~10,000 automatic status emails a month, plus 26 customer logins.
  • Proof of delivery: paper, often lost → signed, photographed, archived PDF (7,300+ so far).
  • Capacity: limited by dispatcher hours → 5.6× more delivery orders in 8 months.
